Product Thinking

Product thinking is a discipline of asking better questions before reaching for solutions — a set of habits for diagnosing problems, making decisions, and learning from what you build. Its three commitments: problems before solutions, outcomes over outputs, and Policy–Ops–Tech integration.

The toolkit

1 · Understand the problem

Great digital products start with problems, not solutions. Most initiatives fail because they solved the wrong problem, not because they executed badly — teams skip diagnosis and arrive with the answer already chosen ("we need a CRM", "we need more AI"). Grab started with taxis, Amazon with books, Netflix with DVDs: nail one problem first, then scale.

2 · Five Whys

A simple tool that moves you from symptom to cause. Ask "why" five times. Problems rarely have one root cause, so aim for causes that are both within your control and linked to the outcome you want.

3 · The 4Cs problem statement

Clarity (what exactly is the problem), Consequence (what it costs), Cause (the root reason), Confirmation (the evidence). A sharp, data-backed statement aligns the team on the right problem — for example, citizens repeatedly re-filling the same forms across government services.

4 · Metrics: SMART, leading/lagging, VCR

SMART — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Leading indicators give early signals so you can course-correct; lagging indicators measure the real outcome after the fact. Value-Cost Ratio (VCR) is the value generated for every dollar spent — the argument for continued investment.

5 · Assumptions and risks

Ask: what could go wrong, and what is the cheapest way to find out? Three risks — market (do users want it), technical (can it be built), team (can we execute). De-risk in stages: Proof of Concept → Proof of Value → Scale → Maturity. A proof of concept does not need to be scalable, it just needs to be testable. Singapore's health appointment booking started as a FormSG form feeding an Excel sheet; its A/B test returned 0 bookings in control against 24 in treatment, with users sharing the link themselves.

6 · A good customer experience

Not a nice-to-have — especially for compulsory services, where citizens cannot go elsewhere. Products that work remove a real, named frustration (ParkingSG, RedeemSG). The 11-Star Framework recalibrates ambition, stretching past the adequate to find the sweet spot between adequate and impossible.
